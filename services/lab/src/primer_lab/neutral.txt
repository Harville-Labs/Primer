A kettle heats water by passing a current through a coil of metal at its base. The coil resists the current and grows hot, and the water above it warms by conduction and then by convection, as the warmer water rises and cooler water sinks to take its place. Most kettles switch themselves off when a strip of two bonded metals bends in the steam.

Bread rises because yeast feeds on the sugars in flour and gives off carbon dioxide. The gas is caught in a network of gluten, which forms when flour is mixed with water and worked by hand or by machine. Baking sets the network in place, and the crust browns as sugars and proteins at the surface react in the heat of the oven.

Rivers carry sediment from high ground to low. Where a river slows, at a bend or where it meets the sea, it drops the heavier grains first and carries the finer ones further. Over many years this sorting builds banks of gravel on the inside of bends and wide flats of silt at the mouth.

A library catalogue records each book by its author, its title, and a subject heading, so that a reader can find it by any of the three. Many libraries also give each book a call number, which decides where it sits on the shelves and keeps books on related subjects close to one another.

The tides rise and fall about twice a day because the moon's gravity pulls on the oceans more strongly on the side of the earth that faces it. The sun has a smaller effect of the same kind. When the sun and moon are in line, the tides are larger; when they are at right angles, the tides are smaller.

Bicycles are steered as much by leaning as by turning the handlebars. At speed, a rider who wants to turn left first makes a small movement to the right, which tips the bicycle to the left, and then steers into the lean. Most riders do this without knowing they do it.

Paper is made from fibres of wood or cotton suspended in water. The mixture is spread across a fine screen, the water drains away, and the fibres bond to one another as the sheet is pressed and dried. The direction in which the fibres mostly lie is called the grain, and paper folds more cleanly along it.

A thermometer of the old kind holds a thin column of liquid in a glass tube. As the liquid warms it expands, and because the tube is narrow, a small change in volume moves the top of the column a visible distance along the scale. Digital thermometers instead measure how the resistance of a small component changes with temperature.

Bees collect nectar from flowers and carry it back to the hive, where they pass it from one worker to another and fan it with their wings until much of the water has evaporated. The thickened nectar is stored in wax cells and sealed, and it keeps for a very long time because so little water is left in it.

Glass is formed when sand, soda and lime are melted together and cooled quickly enough that the atoms have no time to arrange themselves into a crystal. The result is a solid without a regular structure, which is why glass breaks along curved surfaces rather than along flat planes the way many crystals do.

A lighthouse marks a coast or a hazard with a light that can be seen from far out at sea. Each lighthouse flashes in its own pattern, so that a navigator who counts the flashes and times the gaps between them can tell which one it is and work out the ship's position from a chart.

Clouds form when moist air rises and cools until the water vapour in it condenses onto tiny particles of dust or salt. Whether the cloud is a thin sheet or a tall heap depends on how quickly the air rises and how far it can rise before it stops being warmer than the air around it.

A piano makes its sound when a key lifts a felt hammer that strikes one, two or three strings tuned to the same note. The strings pass their vibration to a broad wooden soundboard, which moves more air than the strings could alone. When the key is released, a damper falls back onto the strings and stops them.

Maps show a curved surface on a flat sheet, and no flat map can keep every distance, angle and area true at once. Some projections keep the shapes of small regions correct and stretch areas near the poles; others keep areas in proportion and bend the outlines of the land.

Composting turns kitchen and garden waste into a dark, crumbly material that improves soil. Bacteria and fungi break down the waste, and they work fastest with a mix of green material, such as grass cuttings, and brown material, such as dry leaves, kept damp and turned now and then to let air in.

Railway tracks are laid with small gaps between the lengths of rail, or on modern lines are welded and held under tension, because steel expands in hot weather. Without allowing for this, the rails would push against one another on a summer afternoon and could bend out of line.

A sundial tells the time from the shadow cast by a raised edge, called the gnomon. For the dial to read correctly through the year, the gnomon has to point toward the celestial pole, which means its angle depends on how far north or south of the equator the sundial stands.

Soap cleans because each of its molecules has one end that mixes with water and another that mixes with oil. In washing water the molecules gather around small drops of grease with their oily ends inward, and the drops can then be rinsed away with the water.

Many birds migrate between a summer range, where they breed, and a winter range, where food is easier to find. They navigate by the position of the sun, by the pattern of stars at night, and by the earth's magnetic field, and young birds of some species make the journey for the first time alone.

A clock with a pendulum keeps time because a pendulum of a given length swings at almost the same rate however wide its swing. A small mechanism called the escapement gives the pendulum a push on each swing and lets the gears advance by one tooth, which is the ticking the clock makes.

Rain gauges are simple instruments: a funnel of known width leads into a narrow measuring tube, so that a small depth of rain across the funnel becomes a taller column that is easier to read. Weather stations record the amount at the same hour each day so that one day can be compared with another.

Wool keeps a person warm because its crimped fibres trap a great deal of still air, and still air is a poor conductor of heat. Wool can also take up a fair amount of moisture from the air before it feels damp, which is one reason it remains comfortable in changing weather.

A dictionary lists the words of a language in an agreed order and gives, for each, its spelling, its pronunciation, its meanings and often its history. The meanings are usually written from examples of how the word has actually been used, gathered from books, newspapers and other writing over many years.

Salt has been used to keep food for thousands of years. It draws water out of meat and fish, and most of the bacteria that would spoil the food cannot grow where there is so little water and so much salt. Smoking and drying work in a similar way, by leaving too little water behind.

A canal lock raises or lowers boats between stretches of water at different heights. The boat enters a chamber with gates at each end, the gates behind it are closed, and water is let in or out through sluices until the level matches the stretch ahead, when the gates in front can be opened.
